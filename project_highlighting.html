Highlighting
redesigned.

One colour, one word. That was the whole feature. This is what happened when we finally fixed it.

Context

Users were staying, but they were working around us.

Derwent Innovation is Clarivate's patent search platform. The users are professionals: patent analysts, IP counsel, R&D teams. They build complex search strategies, spend hours inside individual documents, and they know exactly what they need. They are not easily impressed, and they don't change their workflow unless something is clearly better than what they already have.

The existing highlighting tool had one word per colour. That's it. Competitors had moved on, concept-level highlighting with multiple synonyms per group had become the baseline. We were behind on something users touched every single session.

The workaround problem

Some users had already migrated parts of their workflow to third-party tools. They weren't leaving Derwent, they were staying and patching the gaps themselves. That's worse, in some ways.

Two audiences

Power users with years of workarounds. New users arriving with fresh expectations. Both needed to be served. The solution had to feel right for both without being designed for either.

The problem

They think in concepts. The tool thought in keywords.

Patent analysts don't search for one word. They track concepts, "battery" in one document, "electrochemical storage cell" in the next, "accumulator" in the one after that. Same thing, different words. They needed to mark all of those with the same colour, at a glance, across every document they opened.

The tool gave them one word per colour. So they found ways around it. Or they just stopped using it.

"I turn off the highlighting in Derwent. I use Multi Find."

James Okafor. Not an outlier. A pattern.

When someone disables a core feature and replaces it with a third-party plugin, that's not a usability problem. That's a product gap.

Research

18 sessions, just me.

This project started in unusual circumstances. The PM and the UX researcher on the team had both left, and for a significant stretch I was running this alone — owning the research, the design, and the stakeholder communication at the same time. A PM joined later in the process, which helped move things forward into development. But the research phase, the design direction, and the core decisions were mine to make.

I ran all 18 sessions myself. Six internal participants, eight external professional users, four senior stakeholders including a Patent Search Principal Lead and a Director of Patent Operations. I recruited, screened, facilitated, and synthesised. No research support.

The most important fix happened between sessions. Eight early participants failed to find Templates and Add Words, the buttons were icon-only. I redesigned them with text labels overnight. The next five found them immediately. Zero failures. You can only do that when you own the whole process.

"I prefer them done automatically. I don't want another step."

Clara Novak, on query term auto-loading. 14 of 14 said the same.

Design: results level

Knowing which patents are relevant before opening any of them

Research surfaced something I hadn't initially scoped: users needed signal at the results level, before opening anything. The only way to know if a patent contained your concepts was to open it. I added a new column to the results table to solve this.

A new column in the results table. Coloured dots, one per concept group. Filled means it's in the patent. Grey means it's not. Hideable via Manage Columns, zero disruption to the existing table structure. The column reuses the same colour system as the record view, no new visual language introduced.

Design: record view

The Mini Map

Open a patent and the right panel becomes the highlighting interface. A compressed map of the full document sits on the left — always the same height, never scrolling on its own. Coloured marks show exactly where each term appears. On the right, a flat list of individual words with checkboxes: select up to five to display in the map at once.

Process

How it came together

Research reshaped the scope mid-study. I used AI tools throughout, for exploring interaction patterns, getting copy right, generating Figma Make prompts. Every output needed review. The tools made me faster; they did not make the decisions.

01
Problem framing
Analysed product analytics, competitive landscape, and existing user behaviour patterns to define scope and priority.
02
Research & synthesis
18 sessions, solo. Recruited, facilitated, synthesised, and fixed things between sessions.
03
Design iteration
Explored multiple directions, discarded anything that introduced new interaction patterns, converged on solutions grounded in precedent.
04
Handoff preparation
Full responsive specification, Figma file with annotated stories, Figma Make prototypes, and open questions logged for dev review.

On AI in the workflow. It helped me move faster while working alone. Exploring patterns, rewriting copy until it was precise, generating prototyping prompts. But every output needed review. The tool generates options. The designer decides what's right.

Decisions

What I built, and what I chose not to.

Reuse over invention

Every new element reuses an existing pattern. The modal is the existing Edit Highlighting modal with one added line. The results column slots into the existing table. Nothing new to learn.

No matrix in MVP

Tested and valued by a small group. Deliberately descoped. The Mini Map was preferred for individual patent review by every participant regardless of role. The matrix is a separate feature for later.

Concepts, not keywords

One colour = one concept = multiple synonyms. This is how users think. It's also what every competitor had already done. The argument was easy to make.

One global scope setting

Early explorations had per-concept scope selectors and per-column section controls. Both got cut. Too much complexity, and neither pattern existed anywhere else in the product. One global setting in the Edit modal — in the right place.

Where it stands

Research done. Design done. Dev next.

The design is complete. Story writing with the PM is done. A developer presentation is scheduled. The work that remains is build and release — and then alpha testing with a subset of the original research participants to validate the decisions made.
