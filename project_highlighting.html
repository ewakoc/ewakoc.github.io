Overview

How this project was run.

Derwent's highlighting gave users one word per colour with no concept grouping, no document overview, no saved templates. Competitors had moved on. I ran the full project solo 16 sessions, all research, design, and PM and used AI as a structural part of the process at every stage, not a bolt-on at the end.

The problem

Where the problem came from.

Highlighting wasn't a feature someone filed a ticket for. It surfaced unprompted, repeatedly, across customer calls, support sessions, and account reviews. Users weren't asking for a redesign they were quietly describing workarounds: copying terms into spreadsheets, switching to third-party tools for the highlighting step, and returning to Derwent only when they had to.

When we ran a competitor benchmark to understand what was pulling users away, the gap was sharper than expected. Other patent platforms had already shipped concept-level grouping multiple terms under one colour minimap navigation so analysts could see where their highlights landed before reading the full document, and saved templates that persisted across sessions and search types. We had one keyword per colour and no overview. The tools users were defecting to weren't just slightly better. They were a generation ahead.

One keyword per colour

No concept grouping, no synonyms, no shared sets. Analysts building a coherent visual language across dozens of documents had no way to do it inside the tool.

No document overview

No way to see where highlights fell before reading. Every review meant scrolling from page one, on every document, every time.

No saved templates

Every search started from scratch. Analysts who had built consistent highlight logic for FTO or competitive intelligence work had no way to preserve or reuse it.

Competitors had moved on

The benchmark confirmed it: concept-level highlighting, minimap navigation, and template libraries were already standard elsewhere. Users knew it.

Discovery

What discovery research surfaced.

Before touching any design, I ran a first round of discovery with 8 users patent analysts, IP specialists, and searchers who used highlighting regularly. The goal wasn't to validate a solution. It was to understand what they were actually trying to do when they reached for a highlight colour, and where the current tool broke that intent.

What came back was consistent: users weren't thinking in keywords. They were thinking in concepts clusters of related terms that belonged together, that shifted slightly between a pharmaceutical search and a mechanical components search, and that they had to manually reconstruct every single time. The feature wasn't broken. The mental model it was built on was wrong.

Patty · Patent Information Analyst built from discovery interviews, job descriptions, and prior contextual inquiry reports. Every design decision was tested against whether it would work for her.

Getting to an interactive prototype quickly.

Discovery gave us enough signal to move. Rather than spend weeks in static wireframes, I used Figma MakeAI to go from research findings to a fully interactive prototype in a fraction of the time it would normally take. The AI handled the structural scaffolding layouts, component states, interaction flows so I could focus on the decisions that actually required thinking: what the concept grouping model should feel like, how the minimap should anchor to reading behaviour, what a saved template needed to communicate to feel trustworthy.

Speed mattered here because interactivity changes what users tell you. A clickable prototype surfaced reactions that a static design never would have hesitation before a click, the moment someone reached for a feature that wasn't there yet, the instinct to drag something that wasn't draggable. Getting to that fidelity quickly meant getting better data earlier.

"I want to see where my concept terms are before I start reading."

How we personalised the prototype for each participant.

One thing that made the usability sessions significantly more valuable: I personalised the prototype content to each participant's field of work. A searcher from food science saw patents about flavour compounds and ingredient processing. A chemist saw organic synthesis documents. Someone working in medical devices reviewed implant and biosensor patents.

This wasn't cosmetic. When the content in front of you is plausible when the patent numbers, the terminology, the concept clusters all match the work you actually do the session stops feeling like a test and starts feeling like your job. Participants engaged differently. The feedback was more specific. The friction points that emerged were real ones, not artefacts of an unfamiliar domain.

What Figma Make enabled

A fully interactive prototype with working hover states, panel interactions, and realistic document navigation built fast enough to test within days of the discovery findings.

What personalisation enabled

Feedback that was specific to real working behaviour, not generic reactions to an abstract tool. Users flagged what mattered to them because what was on screen already mattered to them.

Personal AI Assistant

Building a personalised Claude assistant for this project.

As part of this project, I built a personalised Claude assistant trained on my research, workflows, and design process. It knew the Derwent user base, the terminology IP analysts use, and how I write acceptance criteria. Every interaction started with the context already loaded.

What I defined

What the assistant should do, what it should never do, and how it should respond to ambiguity. The rules matter as much as the data.

What I trained it on

Session notes, affinity maps, research patterns, user story templates, and my own written feedback from previous rounds, all project-specific.

How I tested it

Against real tasks: could it draft a story I'd actually ship? Did it flag the same edge cases I would? Did it stay grounded in the research?

What I documented

A step-by-step guide for building synthetic users for usability testing, grounded in real interviews, behavioral data, and deployed as a personalised AI agent.

User research

Running sixteen usability sessions and synthesising the findings.

Running 16 moderated usability sessions solo generates a volume of raw material that's genuinely difficult to manage. Observation notes, transcripts, task completion data, verbal quotes, and body-language reads, all from me, all needing synthesis. I used AI not to replace my analysis, but to hold more of it at once than I could alone.

Before running live sessions, I used synthetic users, AI-generated personas built from Derwent's published user research, job descriptions for IP analysts and patent attorneys, and contextual inquiry reports from prior projects, to stress-test my discussion guide and prototype early enough that failure was free. I prompted each persona in Claude with a full task brief: complete these six tasks in the prototype, narrate your first action, describe what you expect, and flag any friction you encounter. The synthetic users surfaced two critical problems before session one: a task instruction that inadvertently primed participants toward a specific panel location, and a prototype screen that skipped a realistic document-loading state. Both were fixed for free, before any real user's time was spent.

Synthetic user session before live testing. The "are my terms saved?" friction appeared across 4 of 5 synthetic personas, later confirmed by real participants in session.

During synthesis: NotebookLM.AI I uploaded all 16 session notes, observation summaries, and post-session survey responses to NotebookLM as a shared knowledge base. Rather than reading through documents linearly, I queried across the full dataset simultaneously: "Which participants mentioned colour as a concept marker rather than just a visual differentiator?" or "List every workaround described for searching with multiple synonyms." The model returned pattern summaries with participant references, showing which findings were represented across 10+ sessions versus mentioned once, which directly shaped prioritisation.

notebooklm.google.com

NotebookLM queried across all 16 sessions, surfacing the concept-vs-keyword mental model split that directly shaped the core data architecture decision.

Sentiment triage: Claude.AI 160+ survey responses tagged by sentiment and feature. The negative cluster around "settings" surfaced the icon discoverability failure before I'd read every response.

Transcript compression: Claude.AI Each session generated ~4,000 words. I fed every transcript into Claude with a fixed template and got a consistent two-page brief per session. I still read everything the briefs were a navigation layer, not a replacement.

Project management

Translating research into developer-ready stories.

Without a PM, translating research into dev-ready stories was mine to manage. I fed Claude my affinity map and friction logs and asked for Jira-formatted user stories with acceptance criteria, flagging any story where a design decision was still open. 24 stories in under an hour. Most needed editing, but the structural completeness was the value things I'd have missed on a first pass were already there.

Claude generated the first draft of the feature backlog, structure, descriptions, dev notes, and acceptance questions. The PM added notes, I refined priorities. The first draft saved a full planning session.

I moved the full story set into Excel and scored each one across three axes: research signal strength (how many participants raised this need), implementation complexity (rough estimate from engineering conversations), and strategic fit (alignment with the win-back objective). The resulting backlog, 8 features across Must-have, Should-have, and Could-have tiers, was both comprehensive and defensible. Every inclusion had an evidence trail.

What Claude gave me

Structural completeness and unprompted edge cases, accessibility states, empty states, error conditions. I had to consciously include or exclude each one.

What I gave it back

Prioritisation and evidence. AI-generated stories don't know what your users actually said. The Excel pass was where research became a defensible strategy.

Usability testing

Testing the design before and during live sessions.

I used a synthetic user, my Claude persona built on real Derwent research, to stress-test the prototype before any live sessions began. It flagged the icon-only configuration control as a likely discoverability issue. I logged it as a risk but didn't change anything yet, I wanted live evidence first.

Eight of the first 14 participants couldn't find the control. After three consecutive failures in sessions 6, 7, and 8, I changed the prototype mid-study: icon-only controls replaced with labelled "Templates" and "Add terms" buttons. The next five sessions showed zero failures on that task. The synthetic user predicted it. Live sessions confirmed it. Having the prediction meant the fix was ready faster.

The synthetic user flagged the icon-only control before sessions began. When 8 of the first 14 live participants confirmed it, the fix was already clear. Changed mid-study after session 8, zero failures in the remaining five sessions.

The Matrix view tells a different story. Synthetic users flagged it as potentially confusing when placed inside a single patent record, they expected to know whether a concept was present in a section, not how many times it appeared. Live participants confirmed this exactly. The Matrix was repositioned to search-results level, where counts across multiple documents carry real analytical value. Same component, different home.

Reflection

Reflections on the process.

Every AI output in this project was material to react to, not material to ship. The AI did the first sweep. I did the thinking. That combination was faster than working alone, and more complete, because the AI's breadth forced me to consciously exclude things rather than just not think of them.

Synthetic users are sharp at structural problems but poor at emotional texture. The hesitation before a click, the apologetic laugh when something doesn't work no synthetic output replicates that. I used them in sequence with live sessions, not as a substitute.

What worked well

Using AI for first-draft breadth and then applying human judgment to narrow. The combination was faster and more complete than either approach alone.

What to watch

Tool-switching overhead accumulates. And synthetic users are sharp at structural problems, poor at emotional texture. Use them in sequence, not as substitutes.
